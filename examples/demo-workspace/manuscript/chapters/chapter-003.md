# Chapter 3: The Collector of Vanishings

The chapel at the Ninth Tide had two arched windows, and the pupils were not white. They were full of evening, and the evening was full of dust, and standing in the doorway with a lantern was a tall man in a surveyor's oilskin, mended with copper wire at the seams, as if his coat had outlived the shop that made it.

"You took your time," he said. "Nine miles. Most people turn back at the third rise."

"Most people," said Isolde, and did not move from the doorway, "turn back because someone carves a threat into the mile-stone."

"That was not a threat. That was a quotation. The Reviser's standing instruction to every surveyor since the Dissolution." The man set his lantern on the chapel table, and the light caught the room: instruments she knew — alidades, a theodolite with a brass sunshade — and instruments she did not, including a press of some kind, a great screw-vise with a platen of gray stone, and stacked beside it, to her sickening certainty, plates. Hundreds of copper plates, face down, like a rookery of dead birds.

"I'm Pell," the man said. "Assistant surveyor. Ninth of that name. My grandmother served with yours, under the same Reviser, and her grandmother before that. We are the Ludlow Survey, Miss Marr, or what is left of it, which is the road you walked in on and this chapel and the plates." He touched one, gently, the way you touch a coffin lid. "The world is printed from somewhere. It has always been printed from somewhere. We are the somewhere."

Isolde had been raised on instruments and understatement. She made herself walk to the press and look at the top plate. It was a chart of a bay she knew — Candle Reach, the safe anchorage every pilot book praised — except that on this plate the bay was a mile wider, and a town stood on the far shore that no pilot book had ever named.

"When they dissolved the firm," Pell said, "they did not stop printing. They started *un*printing. The Reviser found that a place is easiest to remove not with fire but with subtraction — strike the soundings, cease the ferry, let two generations forget the name, and the sea takes the rest. Harrow Deep. Vestry Mouth. The light on Saddle Rock. Eleven places off this coast alone, and more inland than we can count." His voice stayed level, which made it worse. "They are not gone, Miss Marr. They are *uncounted*. The atlas keeps the count. Your grandmother's atlas. Every ninth surveyor keeps it and passes it hand to hand, and the Reviser's people erase, and we—" he gestured at the plates, the chapel, the nine miles of cobbles she had felt solid under her boots, "—we keep the places alive by drawing them. Drawing is remembering with the hands. A drawn thing cannot be fully removed while any copy of the drawing lasts."

"Then they erase the surveyors," Isolde said. It was not a question. Her grandmother had died alone in a house fire that had never satisfactorily explained itself.

"They retire us," said Pell. "From everywhere. From records, from memory, from the parish rolls. My predecessor is a smell of cedar smoke in a shop in Harrow Point." He let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "The parcel came to you because the atlas chooses the hand, and the hand it chose was trained before it could read. The Reviser knows the atlas has moved. That is what the mile-stone meant. You have perhaps a season."

Isolde looked at the press, at the gray platen worn concave, at the mended oilskins and the thin man who had spent his life in an uncounted chapel, guarding copper ghosts of drowned towns because someone had to. She thought of the lamplight in her shop and the ferries that had stopped running the very morning the atlas chose to arrive.

"A season," she said, "is enough to draw the coast."

Pell, for the first time, smiled like a man whose survey has come out true. "That," he said, "is what the last six of us thought. We are all of us still right. We are simply also gone."
