# Chapter 4: What the Ink Refuses

They worked by the rhythm of the tides, because the atlas worked by nothing else.

At low water, Pell locked the chapel and walked the uncounted coast with the field book, sounding and pacing, keeping the hand in practice. At high water, Isolde drew — the harbor at Harrow Deep first, because it was nearest and because her grandmother had spent eleven posthumous pages on it and deserved a finisher's hand. She drew the tide mill with its dripping wheel. She drew the chandlery, the rope-walk, the long quay with its mooring rings worn to knives. She drew the names, which mattered most of all; Pell said erasure began with names and so restoration had to end with them, in lettering clean enough to be read by someone who had never been told to look.

The atlas accepted her work the way calm water accepts a keel. She would ink a line at night and find, by morning, that the paper had taken it into the old brown world of the survey, walnut and iron, as though her grandmother's ghost were holding the page flat. Once — only once — she found a note in the margin in the tall slanted hand. *The quay steps are nine. Count them.* She had drawn eight. She walked to Harrow Deep and counted: nine, the ninth furred with weed, exactly at the waterline. She never made that error again, and she stopped asking how.

The Reviser's answer came in the sixth week.

Isolde reached the shop at dawn after two nights at the chapel and found the door standing open and her drawing table swept clean — not robbed, *swept*, the modern charts squared into a neat stack like a bureaucrat's apology. On the blotter, dead center, lay a single sheet of copperplate paper. On it, in a hand of terrible confidence, was a map of Harrow Point. Her shop was on it. The cliff was on it. The sea was on it.

The Harrow Deep road was not.

*You are a good draftsman,* said the note beneath, *and this is a good town. Choose.*

She understood the choice with a cartographer's certainty. Every place she restored to the atlas, the Reviser would subtract from the world a little faster — another ferry line cut, another ledger burned, another name let go. Drawing was not free. Drawing was a withdrawal against the only account the Reviser respected: the attention of the living. And he was offering to stop the withdrawals, for one small town, if she stopped remembering eleven.

Isolde sat down on her swept table and let herself feel, honestly, the full weight of the arithmetic. Harrow Point was four hundred people, a bell, a brass fitting, a harbor. Harrow Deep was a drowned quay and two ghosts and a man who mended his coat with wire. Any honest actuary would take the bargain.

Then she took out the atlas and turned to the index and read the marginal note her grandmother had left on the first page, the one she had taken for a motto about draftsmanship: *The map is not the country. It is the promise that the country can be found.* The Reviser's whole power rested on the assumption that people do not look for what is not drawn. Fine. Then the work was not to fight his subtraction line by line. The work was to draw so well, so beautifully, so *findably* — copies, fair copies, a thousand plates scattered like seeds — that the looking became inevitable.

She inked her pen. She began, on fresh paper, not with Harrow Deep and not with the chapel, but with a perfect, loving, freely-given chart of Harrow Point itself, the town the Reviser had offered to spare, drawn in fair copy for anyone to keep. Under the neat trident of the Ludlow Survey, where the plate number belonged, she wrote: *Edition of one thousand. Copy freely.*

Some ink refuses to be a bargaining chip. The pen knew. It moved like the tide.
