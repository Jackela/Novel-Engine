# Chapter 2: The Ninth Tide Road

The atlas held four hundred pages and Harrow Deep was on only one of them, but by the third week Isolde had found eleven more places that shared its condition: surveyed, sounded, inked with total confidence, and absent from every living record.

There was a lighthouse on the Saddle Rock that no keeper's ledger mentioned. There was a herring fleet out of a port called Vestry Mouth, with tonnages listed to the half-barrel. There was a road — this was the entry that finally moved her — a coast road running nine miles from the tide mill at Harrow Deep to a chapel at the Ninth Tide, hedged on the landward side and cobbled on the seaward side, with the surveyor's marginal note: *Grade even. Worth keeping.*

Worth keeping. As if keeping were a thing you could decide to do or fail to do, the way you decide to recaulk a window.

She knew the chapel. Not from memory — she had never seen it — but from her grandmother's practice sheets, the discarded exercises of a nine-year-old's lessons: the chapel tower drawn forty times in pencil, then in pen, the pupils of the two arched windows always left white. Her grandmother had taught her letters with its name. *Ninth Tide. Again. Carefully.* Isolde had assumed it was a game, the way children assume the private languages of their elders are games.

On a Tuesday when the weather stood fair and the shop had no customers, Isolde packed the atlas, a folding rule, bread, and the sounding-weight, and walked out past the last house of Harrow Point to where the road ended at the sheep fence.

Beyond the fence the cliff grass ran to the edge, and below the edge the sea stood in the gray light like poured pewter. There was no road. There had never been a road; the survey plates in the county office showed unbroken grass from the fence to the fall.

She opened the atlas on the grass. She read the entry again. *Grade even. Worth keeping.* Then she took out her pencil — the flat carpenter's pencil she used for field notes — and did the only thing a cartographer does when the plate and the world disagree. She corrected the world.

She drew the road. Not confidently. Her hand shook, and the hedge came out at first like a rind of shadow, and she had to lift the pencil and breathe and start the line again where the grass bent. But she drew it as her grandmother had taught her: observation first, certainty after. She drew the cobbles sea-side, the hedge landward, the mile-stone at the third rise with its number rubbed to a ghost.

When she looked up, the road was there.

It did not shimmer. It did not assemble out of light like something in a puppet show. It was simply present, and had apparently always been so: cobbles furred with lichen, hedge in blossom, the sea-wind running along it the way wind runs along a thing that has stood for two hundred years. The only wrongness was in her own legs, which remembered walking through grass, and the sheep at the fence, which would not come near it.

Isolde stood at the head of the Ninth Tide Road for a long time. She was not a coward, but she was careful, and care is its own kind of courage, the kind that gets to come back.

Then she stepped onto the cobbles, and walked nine miles into a place the world had thrown away, and at the third rise she found the mile-stone exactly as she had drawn it — and beside it, chiseled fresh, in letters that were not in her atlas and were not in her hand:

*STOP DRAWING.*
