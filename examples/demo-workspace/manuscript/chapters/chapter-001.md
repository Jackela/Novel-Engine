# Chapter 1: The Inheritance of Coastlines

The atlas arrived on the morning the ferries stopped running, which Isolde Marr would later decide was not a coincidence but a greeting.

She found it wedged against the door of her shop — a brass fitting, a cracked bell, and a counter where she inked charts for fishermen who no longer trusted the water — wrapped in oilcloth and tied with surveyor's twine. No note but her grandmother's handwriting on the label, the letters tall and slanted as mizzen sails: *For the one who still draws by hand.*

Her grandmother had been dead for eleven years. The mail boat had stopped coming to Harrow Point last spring. And yet here was the parcel, smelling of cedar smoke and tidal mud, as if it had been traveling for a decade and had chosen, deliberately, this morning to arrive.

Inside the oilcloth lay an atlas bound in gray seal-leather, its spine stamped with no title, only a small stamped rule and the numeral 1. Isolde knew the binding. Every cartographer on the northern coast knew it. It was the house style of the Ludlow Survey, the firm that had mapped the coast a century ago and then dissolved overnight, its plates confiscated, its surveyors scattered. Her grandmother had never admitted to working for them. Her grandmother had never admitted to anything, which was different.

The atlas opened to an index of coastlines, and Isolde felt the shop tilt beneath her feet.

There, between the Skerries and the Candle Reaches, was an entry she had never seen in any chart, though she owned every chart the Admiralty had printed in forty years: *Harrow Deep, township, surveyed twice, soundings current.* Harrow Deep did not exist. It had never existed. Her town was called Harrow Point, and it clung to the cliff above the water, and below the cliff there was only the sea.

Except the atlas gave Harrow Deep a harbor. The harbor had quays, a tide mill, a chandlery marked with the surveyor's neat trident. The soundings ran to thirty feet. The ink was old, the brown of walnut and iron, but the lines were confident — drawn by someone who had stood on that quay and counted the depth with a weighted line.

Isolde carried the atlas to her drawing table, held a page flat under the lamp, and did what she had done every working day since she was nine years old. She checked the plate against the world.

She checked it with dividers, with a magnifier, with the little lead sounding-weight her grandmother had given her to keep on the sill. The plate was clean. The plate was accurate. The plate was more accurate than any modern chart she owned, and she owned the good ones.

Which meant one of two things. Either her grandmother had spent her hidden years inventing a harbor with the discipline of an honest surveyor — or the harbor had been real, and real enough to be measured twice, and someone had taken it out of the world without taking it out of the ink.

Outside, the storm the ferries had run from came up the channel and put out the light. Isolde sat in the dark with her hand flat on the seal-leather cover, and beneath her palm, so faint she would spend years learning to prove it, the atlas continued to dry.
